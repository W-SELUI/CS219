Question 1 

i. Definition of Cloud Computing

Cloud computing is basically the delivery of computing resources like servers, storage and software over the Internet instead of a business having to own and run its own physical hardware. Instead of Mere buying servers to host her marketplace, she can rent computing power and storage from a provider and only pay for what she uses. Some of the main characteristics of cloud computing include on-demand-self-service (she can set up resources herself without contacting the provider), broad network access(accessible from any device with internet), resource pooling (many customers share the same underlying infrastructure), rapid elasticity (resources can scale up or down depending on demand e.g. during a sale period), and measured service (usage is tracked and billed accordingly), like a pay-as-you-go system)

ii. Advantages of Cloud Computing for Mere's Marketplace

Using cloud computing would allow Mere to avoid the high upfront cost of buying and maintaining her own servers, since she can rent infrastructure instead. It also gives her scalability, meaning as her marketplace grows and more customers or business join, she can increase her storage and processing capacity without needing to buy new equipment. Cloud-base SaaS tools also reduce the technical burden on her, since she does need her own dedicated IT Team to manage servers or softwires updates. Her platform would also be accessible to customers and business owners from anywhere as long as they have internet access, which suits a business trying to reach different islands. Lastly, cloud providers generally offer good uptime and backup systems, giving her more reliability than she could set up on her own with limited resources

iii. Disadvantages and Limitations

Although cloud computing offers many benefits, there are also risks Mere would need to consider. Subscription costs for cloud and SaaS services can add up over time, and may become expensive as the business grows. Because everything runs through the internet, the business becomes fully dependent on having a stable connection, meaning any outage could stop operations completely. There are also concerns around confidentiality and integrity of data, since customer information and payment details are being stored and processed by a third-party provider rather than Mere herself, which raises trust and security issues. Vendor lock-in is another limitation, where switching cloud providers later on could be difficult or costly if the business becomes too dependent on one platform's tools. Finally, SaaS solutions may not always be fully customizable to fit her specific business processes compared to a custom-built system

iv. Considerations in the Pacific Island Context

There are several factors that could affect whether Mere's cloud-based business is realistically feasible in Fiji and other Pacific Island countries. Internet connectivity is one of the biggest concerns, as many rural or outer island areas still experience slow or unreliable internet, which would directly affect access to her platform. The cost of data and internet services in the Pacific is also relatively high compared to income levels, which could increase her operating costs and make the platform less affordable for some customers. Digital infrastructure is another issue, since there are no local cloud data centers in Fiji, meaning most cloud providers host data overseas (such as in Australia or Singapore), which can lead to higher latency. Cybersecurity is also a concern, as the region has comparatively lower awareness and regulation around online security, so Mere would need to rely heavily on the security measures of her chosen cloud provider. There is also a shortage of technical expertise locally, which could make it harder to manage or troubleshoot cloud-related issues. Lastly, natural disasters like cyclones and general power reliability issues in the Pacific could disrupt access to the platform even if the cloud provider itself is stable.

Overall, cloud computing could still work for Mere's business, but its success would depend heavily on stable internet access, cost management, and how well she plans around the region's infrastructure limitations.

1. Software as a Service (SaaS) Model

Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) is a cloud computing model where applications are hosted by a service provider and accessed by users over the internet, usually through a web browser. The provider manages the underlying servers, storage, operating systems, software maintenance, and updates, while the customer mainly uses and configures the application [1].

For Mere’s online marketplace, SaaS could allow her to use ready-made online services for functions such as **inventory management, accounting, customer communication, and order management**. Instead of purchasing servers and installing and maintaining software herself, Mere could subscribe to these services and access them online. This would reduce the amount of technical infrastructure she needs to manage.

This differs from **traditional software**, where applications are normally installed and managed on the business’s own computers or servers. The business may be responsible for installation, hardware, updates, security patches, and maintenance. With SaaS, these responsibilities are largely handled by the provider, which can reduce upfront costs and make software easier to deploy [2].

2. Proposed Online Business

Mere could operate her online marketplace using **SaaS applications accessed through the internet**, allowing the business to use cloud-hosted software without managing the underlying servers and infrastructure [1].

For **customers**, the marketplace could provide product browsing, online ordering, electronic payments, order confirmations, and delivery or purchase updates. For **participating local businesses**, the platform could provide tools to upload products, update prices and stock levels, receive and manage customer orders, track sales, and communicate with customers.

These SaaS services would work together by sharing information across the marketplace. For example, when a customer places an order, the order-management service could notify the seller, update inventory records, process payment information, and provide the customer with an order update. Because the applications are cloud-based, Mere and participating businesses could access these services online without maintaining the underlying cloud infrastructure themselves [1].
